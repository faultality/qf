Revision sheet

Definitions, key ideas and reusable exam methods in one place.

1. Stationarity, white noise and dependence

Weak stationarity

A process is covariance stationary if its mean is constant, its variance is constant, and its autocovariance depends only on the lag.

E(Yₜ) = μ; Var(Yₜ) = σ²; Cov(Yₜ,Yₜ₊ₕ) = γ(h)

Strict stationarity

All finite-dimensional joint distributions are invariant to a common time shift. Strict stationarity does not necessarily imply weak stationarity if the required moments do not exist. For a Gaussian process, weak stationarity implies strict stationarity.

White noise

Zero mean, constant variance and zero autocovariance at non-zero lags. White noise need not be independent or IID.

Key distinction

Independence implies zero covariance when second moments exist; zero covariance does not generally imply independence.

2. ARMA / ARIMA models and roots

Model identification

AR(p): dependence on lagged values. MA(q): dependence on current and lagged innovations. ARMA(p,q): both. ARIMA(p,d,q): ARMA after differencing d times.

φ(L)(1 − L)ᵈYₜ = c + θ(L)εₜ

Lag operator

LYₜ = Yₜ₋₁; LˢYₜ = Yₜ₋ₛ. Use the lag operator to collect the AR and MA polynomials.

Roots

For the standard AR lag polynomial, stationarity requires roots outside the unit circle. For the MA lag polynomial, invertibility requires roots outside the unit circle. The reciprocal characteristic-root convention puts the inverse roots inside; state which polynomial you use.

Finite MA process

A finite MA(q) process is covariance stationary if the innovations are stationary; its MA coefficients do not need restrictions for covariance stationarity. Invertibility is a separate property.

3. ACF, PACF and autocovariance
γ(h) = Cov(Yₜ,Yₜ₊ₕ); ρ(h) = γ(h)/γ(0)

ACF measures correlation with lagged observations. PACF measures the direct relationship after controlling for intermediate lags.

  • AR(p): ACF tails off; PACF cuts off after p.
  • MA(q): ACF cuts off after q; PACF tails off.
  • ARMA(p,q): both generally tail off.

AR(1)

Yₜ = c + φYₜ₋₁ + εₜ;   ρ(h) = φ|h|

For a stationary AR(1), the intended expression is ρ(h) = φ|h|.

4. Forecasts, information sets and forecast errors

Under MSE

Ŷₜ₊ₕ|ₜ = E(Yₜ₊ₕ | Iₜ)

Forecast error

eₜ₊ₕ|ₜ = Yₜ₊ₕ − Ŷₜ₊ₕ|ₜ

Retain innovations known at the forecast origin. Replace unknown future innovations with their conditional expectation, usually zero under the stated white-noise assumption. At longer horizons, additional future shocks enter the forecast error.

AR(1) forecasts

Ŷₜ₊ₕ|ₜ = μ + φʰ(Yₜ − μ),   μ = c/(1−φ)

For |φ| less than one, the forecast converges to the unconditional mean as the horizon increases.

Optimal MSE error properties

  • Optimal forecasts are unbiased.
  • One-step errors are white noise.
  • h-step error variance is non-decreasing and converges to the unconditional variance.
  • h-step errors are at most MA(h−1).
5. Non-stationarity and unit-root testing

Trend-stationary

Stationary fluctuations around a deterministic time trend. Removing the deterministic trend is the relevant transformation.

Difference-stationary

A stochastic trend, such as a random walk, is removed by differencing. A random walk has variance increasing with time conditional on its initial value.

ADF logic

ΔYₜ = δ + γYₜ₋₁ + Σᵢ ψᵢΔYₜ₋ᵢ + uₜ

Null: unit root (γ = 0). Alternative: stationarity under the deterministic specification. The specification may include no deterministic term, an intercept, or an intercept plus trend. Use DF/ADF critical values, not ordinary standard-normal critical values.

6. Box–Jenkins and model selection
  1. Identification: inspect plot, stationarity, ACF and PACF; propose candidate orders.
  2. Estimation: estimate parameters; AR models may use OLS, while MA/ARMA estimation accounts for unobserved innovations.
  3. Diagnostic checking: inspect residual autocorrelation, heteroskedasticity and structural change; compare candidate models and favour parsimony with adequate fit.

Ljung–Box

Q(m) = T(T+2) Σⱼ₌₁ᵐ ρ̂ⱼ²/(T−j)

Null: no autocorrelation through lag m. Small p-value: reject the null; residual autocorrelation remains.

For AIC/BIC, lower values are preferred when models are compared on a consistent sample and convention. Information criteria do not replace residual diagnostics.

7. Optimal forecasts and loss functions
Ŷ* = arg minᵧ̂ Eₜ[L(Y, Ŷ)]
  • Squared-error loss → conditional mean.
  • Absolute-error loss → conditional median.
  • Asymmetric absolute loss → conditional quantile determined by the asymmetry.

Method: write expected loss, differentiate with respect to the forecast, set the FOC to zero, solve, and check the SOC or minimisation condition. Do not assume the conditional mean under every loss function.

8. Mincer–Zarnowitz test
Yₜ₊₁ = β₀ + β₁Ŷₜ₊₁|ₜ + uₜ₊₁

Null: β₀ = 0 and β₁ = 1. Alternative: at least one restriction fails. Conduct a joint test of the two restrictions and interpret the result as a regression-based test of forecast calibration/optimality.

9. Diebold–Mariano test
dₜ = L(Yₜ, Ŷᴬₜ) − L(Yₜ, Ŷᴮₜ);   H₀: E(dₜ)=0
DM = d̄ / √V̂(d̄)

Under uncorrelated loss differences, this can be written as d̄√T / √V̂(dₜ). Rejection means unequal expected loss. With dₜ = loss A − loss B, a negative average difference means A has lower average loss. Account for serial dependence when it is present.

10. Forecast encompassing and combination

Forecast A encompasses B if B adds no relevant information conditional on A. A regression-based test considers the restrictions β₁ = 1 and β₂ = 0 in a regression on both forecasts.

MSEc = ω²σ₁² + (1−ω)²σ₂² + 2ω(1−ω)ρσ₁σ₂
ω* = (σ₂²−ρσ₁σ₂)/(σ₁²+σ₂²−2ρσ₁σ₂)

If σ₁ = σ₂ = σ, the optimal weight is one-half and the combined MSE is σ²(1+ρ)/2.

11. Financial returns and volatility

Lecture 8 summarises stylised facts including weak serial correlation in returns, non-normal unconditional returns, volatility clustering, leverage effects, volatility mean reversion, time-varying cross-correlation and long memory in volatility.

εₜ = σₜvₜ;   σₜ² = ω + αεₜ₋₁²

ARCH models let conditional variance depend on past squared shocks. GARCH adds lagged conditional variance, allowing volatility persistence. The complete extensions lecture is not included in the uploaded slide pack.

12. Value-at-Risk: the course's anchor formula
VaRₜᵅ = −μₜ − σₜΦ⁻¹(α)

This is the normal conditional-distribution formulation shown in Lecture 1. μₜ is conditional mean return, σₜ is conditional standard deviation, and Φ⁻¹(α) is the standard-normal quantile. The lecture itself raises the question of whether normality is appropriate.

Full VaR model comparison and backtesting details belong to later lectures, which were listed in the course outline but not present in the supplied slide file.
13. VAR, Granger causality and cointegration

A VAR is a system in which each variable is explained by its own lags and lags of the other variables. Innovations across equations may be contemporaneously correlated.

Y Granger-causes Z if lagged Y helps predict Z, conditional on lagged Z and the other information in the model. Test the joint zero restrictions on lagged Y terms in the Z equation.

For a two-variable system, the rank of Π in the VECM representation determines the cointegration case. The lecture identifies Johansen's test as the procedure for testing the rank.

Exam technique

A repeatable structure for showing your reasoning and securing method marks.

Universal calculation / derivation template

Use this structure whenever a question asks you to derive, calculate or show.
01 · Identify the model and assumptions
Write down the process, parameter restrictions, innovation properties and information set.
02 · State the relevant definition
For example, weak stationarity, optimality or the null hypothesis.
03 · Set up the mathematical expression
Do not jump straight to a final formula.
04 · Show the algebra
Explain why cross-products vanish, which shocks are known and which are future shocks.
05 · Box the result
Make the final expression easy to identify.
06 · Interpret it
State what it implies about stationarity, model adequacy or forecast performance.

Hypothesis-testing template

Use this order for ADF, Ljung–Box, DM, MZ and Granger tests.
  1. Name the test and the question it addresses.
  2. State H₀ and H₁ precisely.
  3. Write the test statistic.
  4. Substitute the supplied estimates and sample size.
  5. Compare the p-value or statistic with the correct critical value.
  6. Reject or fail to reject H₀.
  7. Translate the decision into the question's context.
Do not write “accept H₀” when the test does not reject it. Say “fail to reject H₀” and explain what the evidence does or does not support.

Common examiner traps

Check these before submitting an answer.

Zero covariance ≠ independenceUse a joint-probability counterexample or explain that uncorrelatedness is weaker.
White noise ≠ automatically IIDDo not infer independence unless it is given or established.
Stationarity ≠ invertibilityStationarity concerns AR roots; invertibility concerns MA roots.
Do not ignore deterministic termsA time trend or alternating mean can make the whole process non-stationary.
Respect the information setKnown shocks remain in the conditional forecast; only unknown future shocks are averaged out.
Check the DM sign conventionWith d = loss A − loss B, negative average loss difference favours A.
Do not assume MSE for every lossDerive the optimal forecast from the loss function in the question.
Model fit is not enoughCompare information criteria and inspect residual diagnostics.
Unit-root critical values differUse the correct DF/ADF specification and critical values.
State extra assumptionsIf independence or a distributional assumption is needed but unstated, make it explicit.

Three exam-ready sentences

Memorise the logic and adapt it to the specific question.

A weakly stationary process has a constant unconditional mean, a constant unconditional variance, and an autocovariance function that depends only on the time lag rather than on calendar time.
Under a mean-squared-error loss function, the optimal forecast is the conditional expectation of the future variable given the available information, and its forecast errors must satisfy specific properties of unbiasedness and unpredictability.
A model should not be selected solely on the basis of its goodness of fit or information criterion; its residuals must also be examined to ensure that the model adequately captures the relevant time-series dynamics.
